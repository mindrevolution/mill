Something is broken in how we build software with AI.

We have models that can write entire applications from a single prompt. We have coding assistants that autocomplete not just lines, but entire features. We have agents that can reason through multi-step implementation tasks. The tooling has never been more powerful.

And yet, the output keeps disappointing us.

## The Vibe Coding Problem

When you sit down with an AI assistant and say "build me a user dashboard," you get a dashboard. It might even look good. It might even work. But is it *done*?

Does it handle the edge cases your users will hit? Does it follow the conventions your team established? Does it integrate with the authentication system you already have? Does it match the design language of the rest of your application?

Almost certainly not — because you never specified any of that. You vibed, and the AI vibed back.

This is **vibe coding**: development driven by loose intent rather than precise specification. It feels productive. You see code appearing fast. But the gap between "working prototype" and "verified deliverable" is enormous, and that gap is where projects fail.

## The Specification Gap

Traditional software engineering solved this problem decades ago with specifications. Before you build, you define what you're building. You write down requirements. You establish acceptance criteria. You create a contract between intent and implementation.

But specifications fell out of favor. They were seen as heavy, bureaucratic, slow. Agile told us to value "working software over comprehensive documentation." And for good reason — the old way of writing 200-page spec documents before writing a single line of code was broken too.

The problem isn't specification itself. The problem is that writing good specifications was expensive and slow. Until now.

## Intent Deserves Structure

What if an AI could help you *think through* your intent before it starts coding? What if the same intelligence that writes code could first help you articulate what the code should do, who it's for, and how you'll know it's right?

That's what mill does. It treats specification as the first and most important step — not as overhead, but as the foundation that makes everything after it faster and more reliable.

> Specifications aren't bureaucracy. They're the bridge between what you mean and what gets built.

mill doesn't slow you down. It redirects the energy you'd spend on rework, confusion, and "that's not what I meant" into clarity upfront. The total time from intent to verified deliverable goes down, not up.

## What Changes

With mill, the workflow shifts:

1. **You express intent** — what you want to achieve, not how to code it
2. **mill helps you specify** — turning fuzzy ideas into precise requirements with testable criteria
3. **The specification drives implementation** — not conversation, not vibes
4. **Verification is built in** — every deliverable is checked against its criteria
5. **The system learns** — observations from each cycle feed back into your project's knowledge base

This is specification-first delivery. It's not new — it's the oldest good idea in software engineering, made fast and practical by AI.
